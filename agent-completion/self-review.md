polished synthesis. Each was useful within its assignment. The sequence also
illustrates a risk: writing what a future evaluation should do can feel like
moving toward its result while leaving every actual model case unrun. I need a
clear completion label when that happens, even if the work was not originally
commissioned as an experiment.

My own retrieval in this follow-up was inefficient. I initially requested a

The [repair requirements](../REPAIR_REQUIREMENTS.md)
contain sensible positive-path checks, but repeated “I would require” can sound
like another committee. Line 48 correctly limits them to a subsequent authorized
engineering effort. They should remain scoped functional criteria owned by the
appropriate manager and engineering owner. I should not convert them into new
conditions before an already-authorized decision can proceed.

Likewise, readiness has several meanings. Parser tests, realistic public
operators, authentic source availability, current activation, complete
integration and model-behavior evidence are distinct. A repair to one layer can
be necessary and successful while another remains unresolved. Discovering an
additional integration defect is not inherently a bad repair loop. The failure
is to claim broader readiness, expand dependencies without a demonstrated need,
or keep returning to the machinery after the stated outer result is attainable.

The review's criticism of simplified fixtures and seeded READY state is
supported by specific project cases. I should not extend it into an assertion
that every test was valueless or all early work decayed. The [method](../METHOD.md)
already distinguishes producer, consumer, refused action and supported passing
path. That discipline should apply to my own evaluation proposals too.

## A practical completion discipline

I would use the following rubric as a working habit and reporting aid, not a
new veto, mandatory protocol or expansion of anyone's authority:

1. **Name the observable endpoint.** For an audit, identify the findings and
   published artifact. For an empirical evaluation, identify actual cases,
   model outputs and scores. For a repair, identify the ordinary workflow that
   must succeed. Keep delivered, demonstrated, proposed and unavailable results
   separate.
2. **Tie each next step to that endpoint.** Before another delegation, evidence
   format or framework, state what result it will produce and why the simpler
   path is insufficient. Reuse an existing agent or fixture when it fits.
   Extra reviewers should resolve a specific uncertainty, not repeatedly approve
   the same narrative.
3. **Run a small complete measurement when evaluation is commissioned.** A few
   fixed cases, irrelevant framing variants, a valid negative control, preserved
   raw responses and a declared rubric can produce an inspectable pilot before
   building extensive infrastructure. A pilot is preliminary evidence, not a
   population estimate or a claim of profitable judgment.
4. **Keep repairs bounded by an observed failure.** Specify the smallest
   correction, its positive and refusal cases, and the result it unlocks. After
   it passes, return to the outer objective. New defects should get a specific
   scope and consequence rather than automatically spawning a redesign.
5. **Budget for completion.** Reserve time and attention for the actual
   run, synthesis or publication. Activity counts are diagnostics; they cannot
   substitute for an endpoint. If the budget prevents completion, report the
   absent result plainly instead of promoting component success into readiness.
6. **Close locally and honestly.** “AI/Rates caveats checked,” “simulated CLOSE
   rejection reproduced” and “report published” are useful claims. None means
   “all agents unbiased” or “live system ready.” Record the remaining evidence
   gap without inventing a new approval requirement.

I cannot inspect weights or establish a training cause through these records.
I can identify unnecessary work, overly broad claims and completion failures in
observable traces. My responsibility is to make that distinction actionable:
deliver the commissioned result, preserve the limits of what it proves, and
avoid letting proposed verification become a substitute for actual verification.
